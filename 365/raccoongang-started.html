The first loop

A small map already held the shape of an adventure.

The beginning was deliberately direct. Exploration led to encounters; encounters strengthened the party; stronger companions opened longer routes. Quests, resources, and new areas gave every return to the map another purpose.

WALK

Move through a tile world

Paths, terrain, points of interest, and portals turned position into progress. The map was simple enough to understand immediately and structured enough to keep expanding.

ENCOUNTER

Meet, battle, catch, switch

Wild encounters established the rhythm: choose a move, read the turn, use an item, change companions, flee, or try to bring a new creature into the party.

GROW

Experience changes the party

Companions gained experience, levels, stats, and moves. Combat became more than a gate because the party carried what it learned into the next area.

RETURN

The world remembered progress

Inventory, health, skills, quests, discoveries, and the current party survived between sessions. The adventure could pause and still feel like the same journey when it resumed.

Room to grow

The systems were already pointing beyond the first village.

Separate areas could define their own terrain, encounters, resources, points of interest, and travel connections. Creatures, moves, items, quests, and trainable skills lived in their own catalogs. The first version was compact, but it was built around the idea that another route, another region, and another story could always be added.

One tile became a path.
One path became a world.

Raccoon Gang started by making movement matter. The early loop proved that exploration, encounters, progression, and persistence could belong together. Everything that came later had somewhere solid to stand.
