Represent / find / decide

How meaning becomes something a system can work with.

The study moved past storing documents as pages of text and into the structures underneath search: turning language into comparable representations, finding useful passages by meaning, and making bounded decisions from patterns.

01 / RepresentEmbeddings

An embedding maps text into a vector: a long coordinate made of numbers whose position reflects learned relationships in language. Similar ideas can sit near one another even when they do not share the same words. That makes semantic comparison possible, but the representation is not knowledge by itself; its usefulness depends on the model, the text being embedded, and the distance measure used to compare vectors.

02 / FindRetrieval

Retrieval begins before a question is asked. Source material has to be cleaned, divided into useful chunks, embedded, indexed, and kept with enough metadata to return to its origin. A query follows the same representational path, then similarity search produces candidates that can be filtered, reranked, and assembled as context. Chunk size, overlap, ranking, and provenance determine whether the answer receives the right evidence or merely nearby language.

03 / DecideClassifiers

A classifier turns an input into one or more defined labels. Some learn from examples; others use rules, similarity, or a language model prompted with a label set. The important work is not only choosing a label—it is defining the categories, handling overlap, setting confidence thresholds, measuring false positives and false negatives, and leaving room for “unknown” when the evidence is weak.

A retrieval path

Six linked decisions between a source and a useful result.

Collect

Keep the source, structure, and origin intact.

Chunk

Divide by meaning without losing necessary context.

Embed

Create comparable vectors with one consistent model.

Search

Return candidates by similarity and metadata.

Rank

Prefer the passages most relevant to the question.

Ground

Carry the selected evidence into the response.

The project was a foundation: learning where retrieval can fail, what a representation can and cannot preserve, and how classification turns fuzzy language into an explicit boundary that can be tested.
